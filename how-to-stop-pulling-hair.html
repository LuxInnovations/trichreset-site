How to stop pulling your hair

Updated 14 October 2026 · By the Trich Reset team · Every claim links to its source below

The short answer

The approach with the most research behind it is habit reversal training:[1] notice the warning signs, give your hands a competing job for about a minute, change the places where pulling happens, and get support. Pair it with a plan for the urge itself. Urges rise, peak and pass, and you don't have to win or lose each one.[2]

1. Find your pattern first

For a week, write down every pull or strong urge: the time, where you were, what you were doing, and what you felt. Most people find two or three situations account for most of their pulling. The ComB model sorts triggers into five areas (SCAMP),[3] explained on what is trichotillomania.

2. Match a tool to each trigger

Trigger-to-tool table
If you pull when…AreaTry
A hair feels coarse, kinked or "wrong"SensoryA textured fidget in the same hand (a smooth stone, a hairbrush, Velcro); run your fingers through your hair without pulling
You think "just one more"CognitiveName the thought as a thought: "That's the urge talking." Delay by 10 minutes
You're stressed, bored or anxiousAffectiveA short guided reset or slow breathing until the wave passes; move your body
Your hand rests on your head while reading or scrollingMotorCompeting response: make a fist or hold an object for 1 minute; gloves or finger tape during high-risk activities
You're in front of the mirror, in bed, or in the carPlaceDim or cover the mirror, keep tweezers out of reach, a hat or bandana in high-risk places

3. Use a competing response

This is the core of habit reversal training.[4] As soon as you notice the urge or your hand moving, do something with your hands for about one minute that makes pulling physically impossible and isn't noticeable to others: clench your fists, press your palms together, sit on your hands, or hold an object. Practise it when you're calm so it is ready when you're not.

4. Ride the urge out instead of fighting it

Acceptance-based therapy treats an urge as something you can notice without obeying.[2] Try this:

  1. Name it: "There's the urge."
  2. Find where you feel it in your body, and rate it from 1 to 10.
  3. Breathe slowly and watch the number. Urges peak, then fall.
  4. Keep your hands busy with your competing response while it passes.

Guided audio can carry you through this when you can't think straight. That is what Trich Reset's free urge track is for.

5. Expect slips, and count the hard days

Recovery is rarely a straight line. Shame after a slip often leads to more pulling. Track the days you showed up, not only the clean ones, so you can see the trend over weeks rather than judging yourself on one night.

When to get professional help

If pulling is causing noticeable hair loss, affecting your work, school or relationships, or you feel low or hopeless, see a doctor or psychologist. A therapist trained in habit reversal training or the ComB model gives you the best-evidenced help. The TLC Foundation lists BFRB clinicians at bfrb.org. If you are in crisis in Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14.

Common questions

How do I stop pulling my hair right now?

Give your hands a competing job for about a minute: clench your fists, hold an object or sit on your hands. Then name the urge, breathe slowly and wait for it to peak and pass.

What can I do instead of pulling my hair?

Use a competing response that keeps your hands busy (a fidget, a smooth stone, a hairbrush), wear gloves or a hat during high-risk activities, and change the places where you pull, such as covering or dimming the mirror.

Why can't I stop pulling my hair?

Trichotillomania is a body-focused repetitive behaviour, not a lack of willpower. Pulling is triggered and reinforced by sensations, thoughts, emotions, habits and places. Tools that target your specific triggers work better than trying to just stop.

Does wearing gloves help with hair pulling?

Gloves, finger tape or a hat are a form of stimulus control used in habit reversal training. They are most useful during predictable high-risk times, like reading, watching TV or lying in bed.
