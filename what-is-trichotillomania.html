What is trichotillomania?

Trichotillomania is a recurring urge to pull out your own hair. Around 1.7% of adults have it. Symptoms, causes, focused vs automatic pulling and diagnosis, with research sources.

Updated 14 October 2026 · By the Trich Reset team · Every claim links to its source below

The short answer

Trichotillomania (trick-o-till-o-MAY-nee-uh), also called hair pulling disorder or "trich", is the repeated pulling out of your own hair, with repeated attempts to stop, that causes distress or gets in the way of daily life.[1] It is a body-focused repetitive behaviour (BFRB), not a habit you lack the willpower to drop. A 2020 survey of more than 10,000 US adults found a point prevalence of 1.7%, with similar rates in men and women.[3]

How it is diagnosed

A clinician uses the DSM-5 criteria.[1] In plain terms, all five need to be true:

  1. You pull out your hair again and again, and it leads to hair loss.
  2. You have tried, more than once, to cut down or stop.
  3. It causes real distress, or gets in the way of work, school, relationships or other parts of life.
  4. It isn't caused by another medical condition, such as a skin condition.
  5. Another mental health condition doesn't explain it better (for example, trying to fix a perceived flaw in appearance, as in body dysmorphic disorder).

Only a doctor or psychologist can diagnose it. If this list sounds like you, that is a good reason to talk to one.

Focused vs automatic pulling

Researchers describe two styles of pulling, and most people have some of both.[5]

The two pulling styles
Focused pullingAutomatic pulling
AwarenessYou know you are doing itYou often notice only afterwards
What drives itAn urge, tension, a feeling to relieve, or a "wrong" hair to removeSedentary moments: reading, scrolling, driving, watching TV, lying in bed
What tends to helpTools for the urge and the feeling: urge surfing, acceptance, competing responsesAwareness first: noticing the setup, then changing the environment

Why it happens

There isn't a single cause. The comprehensive behavioural (ComB) model describes pulling as something that is triggered and reinforced across five areas, often remembered as SCAMP:[6]

The five SCAMP areas, with examples
AreaWhat it meansExample trigger
SensoryThe physical feel of hair, roots, textureA coarse or kinked hair that feels "wrong"
CognitiveThoughts and beliefs"I'll just get this one and stop"
AffectiveEmotionsStress, boredom, anxiety, sadness
MotorHand position and habitsResting your hand on your head while you read
PlaceWhere and whenThe bathroom mirror, bed, the car

Is trichotillomania dangerous?

It is usually not physically dangerous, but it can cause patchy hair loss, skin irritation and a lot of shame. One rare but serious risk: people who swallow pulled hair can develop a hairball in the stomach or intestines (a trichobezoar), which may need surgery.[4] If you swallow hair and have stomach pain, nausea or vomiting, see a doctor.

Common questions

How common is trichotillomania?

A 2020 survey of more than 10,000 US adults found 1.7% currently had trichotillomania, with similar rates in men and women (Grant, Dougherty and Chamberlain, Psychiatry Research, 2020).

Is trichotillomania a form of OCD?

It is grouped with obsessive-compulsive and related disorders in the DSM-5, but it is its own condition. Pulling is often driven by sensation, tension or emotion rather than obsessive thoughts, and in ICD-11 it sits among the body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders.

Can trichotillomania go away on its own?

For some people it eases with time, but for many it continues into adulthood. Behaviour therapy with habit reversal training has the strongest evidence for reducing pulling.

Will my hair grow back?

In most cases, yes. Hair usually regrows once pulling stops, although regrowth can take months, and repeated pulling from the same follicle over many years can sometimes damage it.
