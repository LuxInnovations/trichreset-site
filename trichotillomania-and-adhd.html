Trichotillomania and ADHD

Up to 3 in 10 adults with trichotillomania also report ADHD. Why hair pulling and ADHD overlap, and practical tools that suit an ADHD brain, with research sources.

Updated 14 October 2026 · By the Trich Reset team · Every claim links to its source below

The short answer

ADHD is common in people with trichotillomania. In a study of 308 adults with trich, 15.3% met the clinical threshold for ADHD[1], and in a US survey 29% of people with trich reported ADHD[2][3], compared with about 2.5% of adults overall.[4] They are separate conditions, but impulsivity, boredom and restless hands connect them, so tools that keep your hands busy and your setup simple are a good place to start.

The studies used different methods (clinical interviews, self-report surveys and health records), which is why the numbers vary.

Is hair pulling a symptom of ADHD?

No. Trichotillomania is its own condition, classed as a body-focused repetitive behaviour.[5] Many people have one without the other. But when they show up together, each can make the other harder to manage, so it helps to understand both.

Why they overlap

  • Impulsivity. In the 2022 study, people with both conditions scored higher on every measure of impulsivity: acting on the spot, attention drifting and not planning ahead.[1] The gap between an urge and your hand moving can feel shorter.
  • Restless, under-stimulated moments. Automatic pulling tends to happen while sitting still: reading, studying, scrolling, watching TV, lying in bed.[6] Those are the same moments an ADHD brain goes looking for stimulation.
  • Hands that want a job. Pulling gives the hands something to do and the senses something to focus on. The sensory and motor parts of the SCAMP model describe exactly this.[7]
  • Plans that slip. ADHD makes it harder to remember a strategy in the moment, so tools need to be visible and within reach, not just in your head.

One encouraging finding: in the 2022 study, people with both conditions did not have more severe pulling, or a lower quality of life, than people with trich alone.[1]

These come from habit reversal training, the approach with the strongest evidence for trichotillomania,[9] adapted to be short, visible and hands-on. The practical guide goes deeper on each step.

When to get professional help

If you think you might have ADHD, start with your GP, who can refer you for an assessment. For pulling, a therapist trained in habit reversal training or the ComB model gives you the best-evidenced help, and the TLC Foundation lists BFRB clinicians at bfrb.org. Common questions

Is trichotillomania a symptom of ADHD?

No. Trichotillomania is a separate condition, a body-focused repetitive behaviour. The two often occur together: in one study of 308 adults with trichotillomania, 15.3% also met the threshold for ADHD.

How common is ADHD in people with trichotillomania?

Studies range from about 15% (a 2022 clinical study of 308 adults) to 29% (a 2020 US survey), compared with about 2.5% of adults in general.

Does ADHD make hair pulling worse?

People with both conditions tend to be more impulsive, but a 2022 study found their pulling was not more severe, and their quality of life was no lower, than people with trichotillomania alone.

What helps hair pulling if you have ADHD?

The same habit reversal skills, made short and visible: a fidget kept where you pull, gloves or a hat during long focus sessions, one simple plan, and guided audio so you do not have to remember the steps yourself.
