They work best on repeat.

How the audio is made

Sound designed to
quiet a busy mind.

Under the voice in every session there's a quiet sound bed. Two tones, slightly apart, one in each ear, that your brain hears as a single slow pulse. It's called binaural audio, and it's designed to invite your brain waves to slow down and follow that rhythm.

As your mind shifts out of busy, everyday thinking and into a calmer, more relaxed state, you become more open to what you're hearing. That's the state where the suggestions in each session can really land, instead of being argued with.

Come back to it night after night and you're practising a new response to the urge: notice it, pause, choose something else. Habits are learned, and with repetition they can be relearned. That's the rewiring every session is built around.

At night the pulse runs low, down where the body drifts toward rest, and eases you toward sleep. In the morning it runs higher, up where attention sharpens, so you come out awake and set for the day rather than heavy.

Best with headphones. It still works without them.

The Stillness Protocol

Fifty years of research.
In your headphones.

You already know the willpower version doesn't hold. Hair pulling is a nervous system doing a job, not a character flaw. The Stillness Protocol is the part of the app built on that: longer guided sessions, made for coming back to night after night over weeks and months. Every stage does a specific piece of work, in a deliberate order, drawing on the published work of people who have spent their careers on this.

  • Habit Reversal Training (Azrin and Nunn). Awareness first, then changing the setup around the behaviour, then a competing response. The most researched behavioural approach for pulling.
  • The ComB model (Charles Mansueto, Suzanne Mouton-Odum). Maps why a given person pulls across sensory, thought, emotion, movement and place, then works each one.
  • Acceptance and Commitment work (Douglas Woods, Michael Twohig). Added to the behavioural core so an urge does not have to be won or lost.
  • Subtype research (Jon Grant). Sensory, low-awareness and impulsive styles of pulling each need a different response.
  • Self-compassion research (Kristin Neff). Shame keeps the cycle going. Self-kindness lowers it.
  • Polyvagal theory (Stephen Porges). Pulling is the nervous system regulating its state. Shift the baseline and the pull is needed less.
  • The TLC Foundation for BFRBs. A leading nonprofit for hair pulling and related behaviours.
